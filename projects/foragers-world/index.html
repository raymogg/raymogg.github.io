Foragers world

A reinforcement learning environment written from scratch in JAX. Source on GitHub.

I built foragers world as a way to explore simulated environments in JAX, evolutionary processes and reinforcement learning. The world is designed to be simple by nature (only one control lever, small environment state) to allow a researcher to reason with changes made by the agent. The goal of the RL agent is to control the population of foragers within a given band. Critically, it is important where this band sits, and I found that controlling the population closer to the extinction threshold was much harder for the agent.

Foragers

Foragers are simple creatures. They breed asexually and randomly walk around their grid environment. They have a measure of energy which is depleted when they take a step and when they breed. Breeding splits their energy between them and their offspring and requires a minimum amount of energy to do.

Agent

The RL agent watches that population and gets one lever - a global movement cost. Raising the movement cost causes foragers to burn energy more rapidly and die off. Lowering it allows them to move about and breed more easily. The goal is to hold the population inside a target band for as long as possible, scoring +1 for every step it stays there over a 500–step episode.

Environment

The whole simulation environment is pure functional JAX. Food is randomly placed throughout the gridworld initially, and regrows randomly (Bernouli per tile) to a per tile cap.

Baseline Regimes

The following three regimes serve as baseline with a fixed movement cost. The first example shows a population collapse where the cost of movement is too high so the population simply collapses. The second shows a relatively stable population that oscillates roughly within the required band. Finally we have a boom-bust population. Movement cost is low so the population expands rapidly, consumes its resources, then starts dying out. The population is able to recover as the foragers can move around cheapily and consume the ever increasing food while the population is dying. Learning Population Control

The RL agent uses PPO to learn a control policy. Its observation is a three-dimensional vector of global aggregates (total food, total population and mean energy) with no spatial features or history. The action space is discrete: 12 move costs from 0.0 to 2.75.

The band is a parameter of the environment, so I trained one policy for each of four bands, for 5M environment steps each, and repeated that over five training seeds. Each trained policy is then scored on the same 256 evaluation worlds, and its reward is averaged over them. The PPO column is the mean of those averages across the five seeds, and the fixed cost column is the best constant move cost averaged over the same 256 worlds.

Based on average return over 256 evaluation worlds, PPO outperformed the best fixed move cost in every target band, and it did so for every one of the 20 trained policies (4 bands × 5 seeds). In this setup the wide band is essentially saturated: with a band about 60 foragers wide, the agent scores almost perfectly on average (496 of 500).

Training

Each band trains for 5M steps, which takes about two minutes on a M4 Pro Macbook Pro. I used Adam with a learning rate of 2.5e-4 that decays to zero over the run.

These curves sample from the policy instead of taking its best action, so they sit a little below the scores above.

What did it learn?

The agent learns a very basic threshold controller. As the policy in each band shows, it switches from a low cost to a high cost across the middle of the population band. Where that switch happens depends on the size and location of the band relative to the total population, food and grid settings. Floor control ablations

The floor band (0–30) was the one I most wanted to improve as most of the other bands were quite easily saturated. Holding the population low without hitting extinction was the harder task for the agent to learn.

Two kinds of extinction

Looking at when the baseline agent's populations died, the extinctions split cleanly into two groups: early (within the first 20 steps) and late (after step 20). In the baseline, 56 of the 68 greedy extinctions were early, with a median extinction step of 9.

The early crash comes from the start conditions. All 16 starting foragers have 10 energy, so they all breed at once and the population jumps to about 50 within 2–3 steps, well above the band. The agent responds with its harshest cull and keeps it up while the population falls straight through the band:

The ablations

I tried two changes to the observation state, alone and together:

  • add population growth observation The agent also sees the population's fractional change since the last step. The baseline only sees the current population.
  • Band-scaled population. The population observation was originally population / 256, so the whole floor band spanned 0–0.12 of the input and one forager was a change of 0.004. I replaced it with the population scaled to the band (here population / 30), so the band spans the full 0–1.
  • Band-scaled + growth observation. Both changes together.
setup avg greedy return avg sampled return sampled extinct
baseline 293 ± 36 207 ± 49 73%
+ growth observation 346 ± 65 235 ± 45 63%
band-scaled 341 ± 40 366 ± 28 33%
band-scaled + growth observation 325 ± 178 318 ± 171 40%

Results

Band scaling is a clear win. With the population scaled to the band, the agent can actually tell 5 foragers from 15, and it learns to ease off the cull before the start-up crash hits zero. For the sampled policy, return went from 207 to 366 and extinctions dropped from 73% to 33%, and it was the most consistent setup across seeds. The greedy policy is less clear cut: it stops dying at the start, but ends up dying later in the episode instead, so total extinctions stay about the same.

Adding the growth observation didn't change anything. On seed 0 it looked like it removed the late extinctions, but that didn't hold up across five seeds. Combining it with band scaling also made training less reliable, with one of the five seeds collapsing onto a strategy that went extinct in every evaluation world.

To back these up I used Welch's ANOVA across the four setups, with each seed as one data point. A standard ANOVA expects every setup to have the same variance, which these runs don't (the failed seed makes band-scaled + growth observation far noisier than the rest). It found sampled returns differ between setups, but greedy returns don't.

To see which setups differ, I compared each one against the baseline with a permutation test (Holm-corrected):

vs baseline greedy return sampled return
+ growth observation +52.1, p = 0.30 +28.0, p = 0.44
band-scaled +47.7, p = 0.26 +159.0, p = 0.024
band-scaled + growth observation +31.2, p = 0.80 +110.9, p = 0.44

Band scaling for the sampled policy is the only statistically significant difference (p = 0.024, below the usual 0.05 threshold). With five seeds per group, 0.024 is the smallest p-value the permutation test can give, so it's as strong a result as this setup can show.

Conclusions

PPO with a three-number observation learns a simple threshold controller that beats the best fixed move cost in every band, across all five training seeds. It is near-perfect when the band sits comfortably in the middle of the population's range, and much weaker when the band is narrow (tight) or sits next to extinction (floor).

Caveats

Five seeds give low statistical power. “No significant difference” in greedy return means differences of around ±50 can't be ruled out. Seed-to-seed variation is small in the wide, ceiling and tight bands but large in the floor band (SD about 36), where a single seed can be off by about 50 points. Every run used untuned PureJaxRL default hyperparameters.
